EAF — Frequently Asked Questions

This section explains key concepts and principles of the Equity Adaptive Framework (EAF), supporting consistent interpretation of its evaluative system and operative provisions. It reflects EAF's current structure, including Appendix A — Operational Clauses & Enforcement Extensions and Appendix B — Authority and Jurisdictional Independence.

1. What is EAF?

EAF (Equity Adaptive Framework) is a holistic, human-centered system for evaluating actions, policies, decisions, and institutional positions across legal, corporate, digital, consumer, regulatory, and social domains. It evaluates substantive validity through structured principles including intent, harm, proportionality, accountability, equity, consistency, and systemic impact.

EAF operates independently of the institutions and jurisdictions it evaluates. Under Appendix B, EAF does not require permission, recognition, or consent from an entity in order to conduct an evaluation or assert its evaluative position. Institutional status, claimed authority, established procedures, and existing jurisdiction do not automatically establish legitimacy under EAF.

EAF combines transparent, human-legible reasoning with structured, context-sensitive, and consistency-driven evaluation. Its own evaluations remain subject to scrutiny under EAF's requirements for proportionality, consistency, transparency, and evidential integrity.

2. What does EAF mean by "harm"?

Harm refers to negative impacts on individuals, groups, systems, or capabilities. It includes physical, financial, psychological, reputational, informational, indirect, systemic, and long-term effects, whether already occurring, reasonably foreseeable, or arising through identifiable causal chains.

EAF distinguishes actual harm, potential harm, indirect and systemic harm, omission-based harm, and aggregate harm. These categories support structured assessment of evidence, causation, foreseeability, and impact rather than relying on intuition alone.

3. Is EAF objective?

EAF does not claim to eliminate all value judgments or uncertainty. Its evaluative process makes relevant principles, reasoning, and contextual considerations explicit so that conclusions can be examined for consistency and evidential support.

Context and interpretation remain relevant to applying EAF's principles. However, personal ideological preference, emotional investment, or narrative alignment alone are insufficient grounds for an evaluative conclusion. EAF's External Consistency Requirement supports comparison with prior cases and cross-domain analogues, while allowing reassessment when material evidence, circumstances, or inconsistencies warrant it.

4. Does EAF replace laws or institutions?

EAF does not require the replacement or abolition of existing institutions in order to evaluate their actions, decisions, or claims independently. Its evaluative independence is distinct from whether an institution recognises, adopts, or accepts the framework.

Under Appendix B, no institution or jurisdiction is entitled to automatic recognition of legitimacy merely because of its existence, status, legal authority, claimed jurisdiction, or established procedures. EAF evaluates such claims against its own principles rather than subordinating its conclusions to the authority being assessed.

Where EAF determines that a legal or institutional position directly conflicts or contradicts with EAF, Appendix B4 provides that the position is invalid under EAF insofar as it is relied upon to legitimise, substantiate, or justify the action in question. Institutional status cannot itself restore that validity under EAF.

5. Does EAF slow decision-making?

EAF is designed to be applied proportionately to the complexity, potential harm, and significance of a decision.

It is particularly useful for complex or high-impact decisions where structured evaluation can expose inconsistencies, disproportionate burdens, overlooked harms, or failures of accountability. Routine or low-stakes decisions generally require less extensive analysis. Opportunity cost and burden sensitivity are part of EAF's evaluative principles, so the cost of evaluation itself should be considered in context.

6. Is EAF meant to enforce outcomes?

EAF does not operate through coercive enforcement. Appendix A12 establishes that EAF operates through outcome invalidation and incentive restructuring rather than coercion.

This does not mean EAF is limited to offering an optional perspective or is unable to reach substantive conclusions. EAF independently evaluates the validity of actions and positions under its principles. Under Appendix B5, where EAF's specified conditions are met, it may assert its position de facto rather than merely recognise or defer to a competing institutional or jurisdictional claim.

Evaluation, assertion, and coercive enforcement are distinct: evaluation determines substantive validity under EAF; de facto assertion expresses EAF's position when its conditions are met; coercive enforcement compels compliance through coercive authority. EAF's independent evaluative position does not depend on external recognition or permission.

7. How should EAF be understood in practice?

EAF should be understood as a structured system for evaluating actions, decisions, policies, and claims through consistent evaluative principles. Its application includes examining intent, harm, proportionality, accountability, equity, systemic effects, and consistency with comparable cases.

EAF is not limited to describing decision-making processes. It can reach substantive conclusions about whether a position satisfies its evaluative requirements. Those conclusions must be supported by the relevant evidence and reasoning and remain subject to EAF's own consistency, proportionality, transparency, and evidential-integrity requirements.

8. Does EAF guarantee correct outcomes?

EAF does not claim that every application automatically produces a correct or optimal outcome. Evaluations depend on the available evidence, relevant circumstances, and application of EAF's stated principles.

However, the absence of a universal guarantee does not prevent EAF from reaching substantive conclusions about particular actions or positions. Those conclusions are evaluated according to the framework's principles and requirements for consistency, proportionality, transparency, and evidential integrity.

Under Appendix B, institutional disagreement, non-recognition, or refusal to engage does not, by itself, invalidate an EAF evaluation. Equally, EAF's own conclusions remain subject to scrutiny under EAF. The substantive justification for a conclusion, rather than the identity or institutional standing of the party asserting a position, is central to its evaluation.

9. What is "alignment with EAF"?

Alignment with EAF refers to the extent to which an action, decision, policy, or position satisfies the framework's evaluative principles and applicable operational clauses. Assessment may include intent, harm, proportionality, accountability, equity, consistency, and systemic impact.

Alignment is not established merely by declaring compliance, following a procedure, or possessing institutional authority. It depends on substantive evaluation of the relevant circumstances and evidence. Where a direct conflict with EAF is established, the applicable provisions determine the resulting evaluative consequences.

Closing Note

This FAQ explains EAF's current operative structure and supports consistent interpretation of its principles and appendices. It should be read alongside the main EAF document, Appendix A — Operational Clauses & Enforcement Extensions, and Appendix B — Authority and Jurisdictional Independence.

EAF operates independently of institutional recognition. Its evaluative position is grounded in its stated principles, while its own conclusions remain subject to consistency, proportionality, transparency, and evidential scrutiny.
